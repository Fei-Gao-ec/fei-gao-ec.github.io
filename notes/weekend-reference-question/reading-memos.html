Reading: the model memos

The subagent reading of the memos: definitions, the lag-1 identity, and what the break-even kink was taken to say.

The tex files constrain the question as follows. No new mechanism is proposed here.

1. Definitions

Index. In the model, \(t\) is a successive bet, not a day or a weekend. Before bet \(t\), the bettor learns the return on the previous stake:

```46:48:Model/One_period_model/one_period_model.tex Bets are indexed by \(t\). Before placing bet \(t\), the agent learns \(r_t \ge 0\), the return ratio (prize divided by stake) on the previous bet \(b_{t-1}\). In the data this is \(\mathrm{RR}_t\). The agent then chooses the stake \(b_t \ge 0\).


The same timing is in `Model/Research_memo/research_memo.tex` lines 60–61. The session note’s “race day” is a simulated device (another bet follows on the same day with probability \(\rho\)), not a measured unit (`Model/Session_case/session_case.tex` lines 45–46).

**What the plotted micro data are.** The empirical note keeps four fields from `Panel` and says nothing else enters the plotted object (`Empirical/change_in_bet_by_return_ratio.tex` lines 49–64):

- `cifno`: account
- `key_index`: within-account record index
- `amount_purchase`: stake on the current record
- `amount_prize`: prize on the current record

The unit is the account-record pair. After `amount_purchase > 0`, RR is prize/stake, rounded to two decimals (`round(RR × 100)/100`). The collapse has 8,975 distinct rounded-RR rows. Figures keep the 500 rows with \(\mathrm{RR}<5\), covering 25,781,293 of 26,170,053 restricted observations. Points are unweighted cell statistics (`lines 64–66, 90–106`).

**RR and the bet change in that file.**

```115:118:Empirical/change_in_bet_by_return_ratio.tex
\(\item[\texttt{RR}]\) \(x/b\), then rounded to two decimal places.
\(\item[\texttt{ChangeInBetLevel}]\) \(b^{\mathrm{lead}}-b\).
\(\item[\texttt{ChangeInBetRatio}]\) \((b^{\mathrm{lead}}-b)/b\).

Level statistics are “in the same units as the stake”; RR and the ratio are dimensionless (lines 111–113). The modeling notes later treat those units as yen.

The join that builds lead_bet attaches the stake from key_index \(t-1\); the join from key_index \(t+1\) is named lag_bet and is not used in the plotted extract (lines 79–86). The note never says whether a higher key_index is later in time. The research memo reads the same objects as: amount_purchase is the stake that produced \(\mathrm{RR}_t\), and lead_bet is the next stake (research_memo.tex lines 230–232). Under that reading, ChangeInBetLevel is \(b_t - b_{t-1}\) conditional on the return of \(b_{t-1}\).

The lag figures the models match are a second object. They are cell means of \(y = b_t - b_{t-k}\) against \(x = r_t - r_{t-k}\), \(x\) rounded to 0.01 and \(|x|<5\), split into four cases: both returns zero; only \(r_t=0\); only \(r_{t-k}=0\); neither zero (research_memo.tex lines 60–61; one_period_model.tex lines 204–227, which uses \(k=2,3,4\) only).

Lottery margins, called the race-level panel. \(S=8{,}975\) distinct ratios rounded to 0.01; \(\Pr(r=0)=0.406\), \(\Pr(r<1)=0.801\), \(\mathbb{E}[r]=0.695\) (one_period_model.tex lines 57–61). Returns are assumed i.i.d. and independent of the stake in the model (lines 57–58). Serial dependence is listed as an open check, not a result (research_memo.tex lines 233–234).

2. Lag 1 versus lags \(k\ge 2\), and the mechanical stake

Reported shapes.

Lags 2, 3, and 4 are the same S: steep on \((-1,1)\), flat outside. Case (3) rises up to break-even and is flat beyond; case (2) is its mirror. The kink is at \(\mathrm{RR}=1\) (research_memo.tex lines 63–64).

Lag 1, in the \((x,y)\) figure, differs: a bump near \(x\approx -0.4\), a trough near \(x\approx 0.3\), and a steep rise on \(x\in(0.5,1.5)\). Relative to the “lost both times” cell, case (2) lies above near zero and case (3) lies below, with a trough near break-even (lines 65–66).

The race-level curve \(\mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-1}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]\) is a separate V: trough at \(\mathrm{RR}=0.9\), \(-6{,}800\) yen (research_memo.tex line 66; Model/Lag1_case/lag1_case.tex lines 47–48). The lag-1 note also says bets rise after a total loss, fall most near break-even, and recover for large returns. Fitting the one-period stake rule to that curve gives \(R^2=0.11\) with the wrong sign (lag1_case.tex lines 47–48).

Other reported data features: the both-zero cell is negative and grows with the lag, \(-534\), \(-763\), \(-1{,}405\), “read as lags 1, 2, 3” (research_memo.tex line 67). After a big win, bets stay high for several bets (case (2), far left; line 68; one_period_model.tex lines 259–261). The exact cell \(\mathrm{RR}=1.00\) is much less negative than its neighbors; the lag-1 note says that cell includes refunds on scratched runners (lag1_case.tex lines 145–146). The data-definition note does not mention refunds.

Identity, then the independence argument. By definition, for every \(k\ge 1\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-k}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]=\mathbb{E}[b_t\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]-\mathbb{E}[b_{t-k}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]. \]

Here \(b_{t-1}\) is the stake that produced \(\mathrm{RR}_t\), and \(b_t\) is the next stake. The difference between the lag-1 curve and the lag-\(k\) curve is

\[ \mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-1}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]-\mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-k}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]=\mathbb{E}[b_{t-k}-b_{t-1}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]. \]

The next-stake term \(\mathbb{E}[b_t\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]\) cancels. A gap between those two curves, as a function of \(\mathrm{RR}_t\), is a statement about lagged stake levels given the return. It is the size of the bet behind the return, together with earlier stakes, conditional on that return.

Proposition 2 adds independence and then gets a constant gap (research_memo.tex lines 161–164):

```161:164:Model/Research_memo/research_memo.tex \(\item\) \(\emph{Curve by last return.}\) If \(r_t\) is independent of everything determined before bet \(t-1\) was resolved, including \(b_{t-1}\), then for every \(k \ge 1\), \(\mathbb{E}[b_t - b_{t-k} \mid r_t] = \mathbb{E}[b_t \mid r_t] - \mathbb{E}[b_{t-k}]\). This holds for any reference rule, any memory, and stakes that depend on past stakes. A lag-1-specific V therefore measures \(\mathbb{E}[b_{t-1} \mid \mathrm{RR}_t]\), the size of the bet behind each return.


The organizing paragraph says the same thing for any preference, including time inconsistency (`lines 53–55`): if a bet’s return is independent of its stake and of the state in which it was placed, then \(\mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-k}\mid r_t]\) is the same curve at every lag, up to a constant. Features present at every lag come from how \(b_t\) reacts to \(r_t\). A feature present at lag 1 only has to come from the return side: who placed the bet behind \(r_t\), or on which ticket.

The lag-1 note states the same link directly (`lag1_case.tex` lines 45–49, 149): at lag 1, \(b_{t-1}\) is the stake of the bet that produced \(r_t\); any change to the stake policy \(h\) appears at every lag; “the lag-1 pattern in this case comes from who placed the bet behind \(r_t\), not from a reaction to \(r_t\).” The research memo’s verdict on that fit is: “The lag-1 V is composition: nobody cuts the stake in reaction to a near-break-even return” (`research_memo.tex` line 133).

The direct split the memo asks for, and has not reported as done, is (`lines 230–232`): mean of `amount_purchase` (stake that produced \(\mathrm{RR}_t\)) and mean of `lead_bet` (next stake), by \(\mathrm{RR}_t\). A peak near break-even in the first is the stake-return link. A V in the second is a reaction of the next stake.

This proposition is about conditioning on \(r_t\) alone. It is not the claim that the \((x,y)=(r_t-r_{t-k},\, b_t-b_{t-k})\) scatter is the same object. At lag 1 that scatter puts \(b_{t-1}\) in \(y\) and \(r_{t-1}\) in \(x\).

Independence is an assumption. The memo lists \(\Pr(r_{t+1}=0\mid r_t)\) and \(\Pr(r_{t+1}>5\mid r_t)\) as checks; under i.i.d. they are flat at the 0.406 total-loss base rate (`lines 233–234`).

## 3. What the kink at \(\mathrm{RR}=1\) is taken to say

```63:64:Model/Research_memo/research_memo.tex
\(\item\) \(\emph{Lag ladder.}\) Lags 2, 3 and 4 give the same S: steep inside \((-1, 1)\), plateaus outside. Case (3) rises up to break-even and is flat beyond; case (2) is its mirror image. The kink sits at break-even (\(\mathrm{RR} = 1\)), which points to a fixed break-even reference rather than a slowly updating one.

The one-period note’s fixed-reference policy is flat for every \(r\ge 1\), increasing on \([0,1]\), with a kink at break-even (one_period_model.tex lines 146–150). The history channel written as \(R_t=(1-r_t)b_{t-1}\) produces a reversed S, “the opposite sign of the data” (lines 165–171; bottom line at research_memo.tex line 48).

An updating rule that mixes break-even with the previous return gets the lag ladder (lags \(k\ge 2\) identical, lag 1 different) and fails the twist and the V (research_memo.tex lines 49–52, 161–170). Proposition 3: for any such weights, case (2) has \(y\le 0\) and case (3) has \(y\ge 0\), because a total loss is always the largest shortfall. “The data have the reverse signs” (lines 165–166). The simulation gives a rising curve by last return, so \(R^2=0\) against the V (lines 168–170).

4. Kőszegi–Rabin, expectations, and the test already proposed

Kőszegi–Rabin is named once, as one of three routes from prospect theory to time inconsistency, and is not the route they simulate (research_memo.tex lines 175–181):

```175:181:Model/Research_memo/research_memo.tex \(\item\) \(\emph{Probability weighting}\) (Barberis, 2012). ... \(\item\) \(\emph{Expectations-based reference points}\) (Kőszegi and Rabin, 2009; Pagel, 2017). \(\item\) \(\emph{Explicit present bias}\) (\(\beta\)-\(\delta\) discounting). Route 1 is the natural one because our model already has probability weighting. A one-period model cannot be time-inconsistent; a horizon such as a race day is needed.


The session note repeats that expectations-based reference points “generate a related time inconsistency,” after a Barberis (2012) plan-versus-action setup (`session_case.tex` lines 61–67). No Kőszegi–Rabin test is specified.

The reference-updating exercise they did run is the \(\theta^{\pm}\) mix of break-even and the previous return, not an expectations-based reference (`research_memo.tex` lines 149–170). The hot/cool discussion says a reference that depends only on \(r_{t-1}\) would make lag 1 differ while keeping lags 2–4 identical, and then says lag order cannot test “hot” versus “cool” (`lines 137–145`):

```141:144:Model/Research_memo/research_memo.tex
\(\item\) lag order is not time since the result: at every lag \(b_t\) is the bet placed right after \(r_t\), so lag 1 cannot be ``hot'' and lag 2 ``cool''; the time gap between a result and the next bet is needed;
\(\item\) risk-loving may show in the odds rather than the stake;
\(\item\) lags 2 to 4 show stakes cut after shortfalls, so any hot chasing in stakes must be confined to short gaps or to odds.

Tests they propose (research_memo.tex lines 229–235; session_case.tex lines 115–118):

  • Compare \(\mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-2}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]\) with the lag-1 curve. If lag 2 has no V, “neither reference updating nor hot integration drives the lag-1 V.”
  • Separate mean amount_purchase and mean lead_bet by \(\mathrm{RR}_t\).
  • Check serial dependence of total losses and of \(r>5\).
  • If dates or race times exist: same-day versus different-day lag-1 pairs, the time gap from result to next bet, and whether stakes rise with position in the day after losses.
  • The session model’s three predictions: the lag-1 twist lives in within-day pairs; mean bet change by \(\mathrm{RR}_t\) is the same at lags 1 and 2; stakes rise with position in the day after losses.

Model/Usual_history_case/prospect_reference_state.tex does state a reference-updating idea. The reference is a state, \(R_{t+1}=g(G_t,R_t)\) (lines 55–68). The simplest rule sets the reference of bet \(t\) equal to the previous bet’s gross win, \(R_t=G_{t-1}\). Other rules in lines 319–332 include break-even on the last bet, \(g(G,R)=-G\), session break-even, and adaptive \(g=(1-\eta)R+\eta G\). That file does not discuss the kink, Kőszegi–Rabin, or a test on the lag figures.

5. Weekend aggregation

Weekend aggregation is not mentioned. “Weekly” appears once, as an unresolved possibility about which sample the photos use (research_memo.tex line 235): “race-level or weekly; weekly bundles would move the lag-1 trough toward \(0.3\).” “Race day” is the session model’s horizon and a proposed split if dates exist.

6. What these files say the data cannot see

The plotted extract contains stake, prize, account, and key_index only (change_in_bet_by_return_ratio.tex lines 49–64). From that, the memos treat as unobserved, or as not yet checked:

  • Clock time, within-race order, and the gap between a result and the next bet. Lag order is explicitly not that gap (research_memo.tex lines 141–143, 233–234). Dates and race times are conditional: “If dates or race times exist.”
  • Plans, intentions, and expectations. The session model contrasts a morning plan with the stake actually placed (session_case.tex lines 61–66). The data note records placed stakes and prizes.
  • Ticket or odds except as they are embedded in the realized ratio. The lag-1 model imposes the short-odds/long-odds split (lag1_case.tex lines 62–68). The memo says risk-loving may show in the odds (research_memo.tex line 143).
  • The reference point itself.
  • Whether successive key_index values are the same race, the same day, or a later day.
  • Bets that were not placed. The RR sample requires amount_purchase>0. A zero-stake indicator is defined and then dropped (change_in_bet_by_return_ratio.tex lines 90, 130).

Contradictions and unresolved gaps

lead_bet versus time. The data note’s arithmetic is: lead_bet on record \(t\) is the stake at key_index \(t-1\) (change_in_bet_by_return_ratio.tex lines 83–86). It never states the time order of key_index. The research memo calls lead_bet the next stake (research_memo.tex line 231). Those agree only if key_index decreases as time moves forward. If key_index increases with time, the plotted change is the previous stake minus the current stake, against the current record’s own return, and the sign relative to \(\mathbb{E}[b_t-b_{t-1}\mid\mathrm{RR}_t]\) flips. A later script comment outside this tex list, Extracte_data/20261026/lag_by_rr_20261026.R line 19, says the next bet is key_index - 1, which matches the memo.

Two curves, two troughs. The race-level V trough is at \(\mathrm{RR}=0.9\) (\(-6{,}800\) yen). The pooled lag-1 \((x,y)\) trough is at \(x\approx 0.3\), while case (3) in that split is near \(x=0.9\) (lag1_case.tex lines 142–144; research_memo.tex lines 65–66, 131). The lag-1 note says the two “may come from different samples.” The memo says the pooled and faceted figures look inconsistent at \(x=-5\), and that weekly bundles would move the lag-1 trough toward 0.3 (research_memo.tex line 235). The empirical tex defines only the by-RR collapse, not the \((r_t-r_{t-k})\) figures.

“Last outcome” is two different rules. The bottom line says moving the reference by the last outcome predicts the mirror image (research_memo.tex line 48). The equation that delivers that reversed S is the unrecovered shortfall \(R_t=(1-r_t)b_{t-1}\) (one_period_model.tex lines 165–171). In prospect_reference_state.tex lines 327–333, “last outcome” is \(R_t=G_{t-1}\), and “break-even on the last bet” is \(g=-G\); those two “move the reference in opposite directions.”

Units and labels the memos treat as settled. Yen amounts (\(-6{,}800\), about \(2{,}500\) yen, the drift cells) are in the modeling notes. The data note says only “the same units as the stake.” The both-zero drift figures are “read as” lags 1, 2, and 3, three numbers for four lags (research_memo.tex line 67). The refund interpretation of \(\mathrm{RR}=1.00\) is in the lag-1 note only.
