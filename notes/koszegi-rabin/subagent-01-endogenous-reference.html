Subagent: endogenous reference

Reading for question 1. How the reference is set, and what actually produces gain-loss.

Gain-loss behavior is an assumed preference, not something equilibrium creates. The reference point is the person’s own recent probabilistic beliefs, so which comparisons count as gains or losses is endogenous. The sensation itself is the Kőszegi–Rabin version of the Kahneman–Tversky value function.

A. How the reference point is determined

Claim. In the 2006 model the reference point is endogenous: it is the person’s rational expectations, held in the recent past, about outcomes. Those expectations are a personal equilibrium: the distribution the person expects must be the one generated by behavior that is optimal given that same distribution as the reference.

“We assume that a person’s reference point is her rational expectations held in the recent past about outcomes, which are determined in a personal equilibrium by the requirement that they must be consistent with optimal behavior given expectations.” (Kőszegi and Rabin 2006, p. 1133)

“our model makes the extreme assumption that the reference point is fully determined by the expectations a person held in the recent past. Specifically, a person’s reference point is her probabilistic beliefs about the relevant consumption outcome held between the time she first focused on the decision determining the outcome and shortly before consumption occurs.” (2006, p. 1141)

The reference is lagged beliefs, not beliefs at the moment of consumption. If someone learns five minutes ahead that a long-expected $100 will not arrive, she updates her beliefs immediately, but “she will still five minutes later assess not getting the money as a loss” (2006, p. 1141, n. 9).

Where the reference is taken as given. Three cases are explicit.

  1. Section II defines utility for an arbitrary, possibly stochastic, reference before endogenizing it. The authors separate “preferences over departures from posited reference points” from “the nature of reference points themselves” (2006, p. 1141).

  2. Inside a personal equilibrium, the person treats the reference generated by her expectations as fixed and then maximizes. Endogeneity is the fixed-point requirement, not a re-optimization of the reference at the moment of choice: she predicts the environment and her own reaction, “and taking the reference point generated by these expectations as given, in each contingency maximizes expected utility” (2006, p. 1143).

  3. Rational expectations are a modeling choice, not part of the utility function. “Our model of how utility depends on expectations could be combined with any theory of how these expectations are formed” (2006, p. 1143). The 2007 paper uses the exogenous case directly: “surprise” decisions are “situations where expectations are given exogenously to the actual choice set” (Kőszegi and Rabin 2007, p. 1048). If she had expected to keep $0, “our model makes the same prediction as prospect theory” (2007, p. 1048).

The environment distribution (prices, choice sets) is an input. What is solved for is the plan, hence the outcome distribution that becomes the reference. Footnote 13 (2006, p. 1145) says the timing of “first focus,” which fixes which beliefs count as recent, is an interpretational input, not derived.

B. How gain-loss utility is defined

Overall utility. For a riskless outcome \(c\) and reference \(r\),

\[ u(c \mid r) = m(c) + n(c \mid r). \]

\(m(c)\) is consumption utility (“intrinsic” utility in the introduction). \(n(c \mid r)\) is gain-loss utility (2006, p. 1138). Both are separable across dimensions: \(m(c) = \sum_k m_k(c_k)\) and \(n(c \mid r) = \sum_k n_k(c_k \mid r_k)\).

The gain-loss function. Gain-loss in dimension \(k\) depends only on the change in consumption utility (2006, p. 1139):

\[ n_k(c_k \mid r_k) = \mu\bigl(m_k(c_k) - m_k(r_k)\bigr). \]

\(\mu\) is a “universal gain-loss function.” Assumptions A0–A4 “correspond to Kahneman and Tversky’s [1979] explicit or implicit assumptions about their ‘value function’” (2006, p. 1139):

  • A0: continuous, \(\mu(0) = 0\), twice differentiable except at 0.
  • A1: strictly increasing.
  • A2 (loss aversion, large stakes): if \(y > x > 0\), then \(\mu(y) + \mu(-y) < \mu(x) + \mu(-x)\).
  • A3 (diminishing sensitivity): the marginal gain-loss sensation is larger for changes close to the reference than for changes farther away. The printed text layer of the 2006 PDF states this with strict second derivatives, \(\mu''(x) < 0\) for \(x > 0\) and \(\mu''(x) > 0\) for \(x < 0\). A later visual read of the same line was ambiguous between strict and weak inequalities. The 2007 and 2009 papers say these inequalities “are most realistically considered strict” (2007, p. 1051; 2009, p. 915).
  • A4 (loss aversion, small stakes): \(\mu'_-(0)/\mu'_+(0) \equiv \lambda > 1\).

They also use A3′, \(\mu''(x) = 0\) for \(x \neq 0\), to shut down diminishing sensitivity. In the shopping application (2006, p. 1146),

\[ \mu(x) = \eta x \text{ for } x > 0, \qquad \mu(x) = \eta\lambda x \text{ for } x \le 0, \]

with \(\eta > 0\) the weight on gain-loss utility and \(\lambda > 1\) the coefficient of loss aversion.

Stochastic reference. If the outcome is drawn from \(F\) and the reference is a fixed bundle \(r\) (2006, p. 1137),

\[ U(F \mid r) = \int u(c \mid r)\, dF(c). \tag{1} \]

If the reference itself is a distribution \(G\) (2006, p. 1137),

\[ U(F \mid G) = \iint u(c \mid r)\, dG(r)\, dF(c). \tag{2} \]

That is the “compare each outcome to each possible reference point, weighted by probabilities” construction. The prose says the gain or loss from a realized outcome “derives from comparing it with all outcomes possible under the reference lottery.” Their example: if the reference is a gamble between $0 and $100, an outcome of $50 “feels like a gain relative to $0, and like a loss relative to $100, and the overall sensation is a mixture of these two feelings” (2006, pp. 1137–1138). Losses in that mixture count more than gains (2006, p. 1138, n. 3). This is not Sugden’s (2003) state-by-state comparison; each outcome is compared with every outcome in the support of the reference lottery (2006, p. 1138, n. 4). Probabilities enter linearly. The paper sets aside nonlinear probability weighting (2006, p. 1137, n. 2; 2007, p. 1050).

Ex ante and ex post. Both.

  • The sensation is attached to the realized outcome, evaluated against lagged beliefs. Equation (2) is how that outcome feels.
  • The person chooses by maximizing expected reference-dependent utility, so she anticipates those sensations. For a sure outcome equal to the reference, \(u(c \mid c) = m(c)\), so “ex ante expected gain-loss utility is zero for deterministic outcomes.” For a nondegenerate lottery it is negative, “because losses from comparing an outcome to a counterfactual loom larger than the symmetric gains” (2006, p. 1145).
  • Preferred personal equilibrium selects, among plans she would actually follow, the one with the highest ex ante expected utility (2006, pp. 1144–1145).

In a fully deterministic environment, preferred personal equilibrium therefore maximizes \(m(c)\) only. Loss aversion “does not affect behavior” and “does not affect welfare” (2006, p. 1146, Proposition 3). It matters once there is uncertainty.

C. Endogenous reference versus endogenous coding of the sensation

Two separate ingredients:

(1) The preference primitive. People feel gains and losses relative to a reference. That is \(\mu\), with \(\lambda > 1\) and diminishing sensitivity. The paper imports it from Kahneman and Tversky (1979). It is an assumption about utility. Personal equilibrium does not derive it. The abstract’s line that gain-loss utility is “derived from standard ‘consumption utility’” (2006, p. 1133) means the functional link \(n_k = \mu(m_k(c_k) - m_k(r_k))\), once \(\mu\) has been specified. The same point is methodological: “a full specification of \(\mu(\cdot)\) allows us to derive both gain-loss utility and the reference point itself from consumption utility and the economic environment” (2006, p. 1136). \(\mu\) is the extra primitive. Without it, rational expectations alone do not produce gain-loss behavior. When the person gets exactly the sure thing she expected, gain-loss utility is zero.

(2) The endogenous reference. Recent rational expectations decide which comparisons are gains and which are losses. At the moment of a near-term choice, that reference is already fixed. Equilibrium only requires that the expectations be consistent with the behavior they induce.

What produces the gain-loss behavior is (1). Ingredient (2) chooses the benchmark.

If expectations were exogenous, gain-loss utility would remain in full. Section II is exactly that object: utility given a posited reference. The 2007 “surprise” case is the same object with an exogenous reference, and it reproduces prospect theory when that reference is the status quo (2007, p. 1048). What would drop out is the fixed point: the prediction that the reference equals the distribution generated by optimal behavior. That fixed point is what delivers the paper’s new comparative statics, including the attachment effect (willingness to pay rises in the expected probability of purchase) and the comparison effect (willingness to pay falls in the price expected conditional on purchase) (2006, pp. 1133, 1136).

Relative to Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and Tversky and Kahneman (1991). The value-function properties are kept. What is added is:

  • Overall utility is consumption utility plus gain-loss utility, not a value function defined only on gains and losses (2006, p. 1138).
  • Gain-loss is \(\mu\) of the change in consumption utility, separable by dimension.
  • The reference is recent probabilistic beliefs, not the status quo as such. The status quo matters when people expect to keep it. The endowment effect among owners who expect to keep the object is loss aversion; the paper predicts it “will disappear among sellers and buyers in real-world markets who expect to trade” (2006, pp. 1134–1135). The “boundaries” in Tversky and Kahneman (1991) and Novemsky and Kahneman (2005), that budgeted spending and planned inventory reductions are not coded as losses, are treated as boundaries of the endowment effect, because those parties expect to exchange (2006, p. 1142, n. 11).
  • A stochastic reference is evaluated by integrating over every outcome–reference pair, equation (2).
  • Probabilities are linear, “contrary to Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and its extensions” (2007, p. 1050). The authors say this will get some predictions wrong, including insurance of low-probability losses.
  • The reference is tied down by rational expectations in personal equilibrium, rather than left as an outside parameter. They describe this as the first formalization of that idea with an endogenous rule for any environment (2006, p. 1135, n. 1).

D. What 2007 and 2009 add about this coding

2007 treats gain-loss as a preference, not as a belief bias. The paper “study[s] preferences over monetary risk” (2007, p. 1047) using the 2006 utility function. Probability weighting is described as part of prospect theory and then excluded: preferences are linear in probabilities (2007, pp. 1047, 1050). Beliefs are assumed correct. The reference is still “recent beliefs,” and “as an imperfect but at the same time disciplined and largely realistic first pass, we assume that a person correctly predicts her probabilistic environment and her own behavior” (2007, p. 1048).

The new solution concepts are timings of the same preference. When the choice is made shortly before outcomes, past expectations fix the reference: unacclimating personal equilibrium (UPE), and preferred personal equilibrium (PPE) as the favorite UPE (2007, pp. 1049, 1056). When the person commits long before outcomes, the choice itself becomes the reference: choice-acclimating personal equilibrium (CPE), defined by \(U(F \mid F) \ge U(F' \mid F')\) for every alternative \(F'\) in the choice set (2007, p. 1058). The paper says these “are not different theories of what outcomes people prefer. … Rather than reflecting different notions of reference-dependent utility, the two concepts are motivated by the same theory of preference” (2007, p. 1059). CPE is the case in which there is “no sensation of gain or loss” from a fully expected payment, because the reference has adjusted to it (2007, p. 1059).

The rationality caveats in Section VI are about bracketing and about underestimating how fast the reference will adjust. They do not reclassify \(\mu\) as a distortion of beliefs. The authors write that “the hedonic effects of choice include both gain-loss sensations and consumption utility” (2007, p. 1062).

2009 repeats the preference assumption and relocates the sensation onto changes in beliefs. Instantaneous utility in period \(t\) is consumption utility plus a weighted sum of gain-loss terms from the change in beliefs between \(t-1\) and \(t\), about current and future consumption (2009, p. 912, equation (1)). “Contemporaneous” gain-loss compares current consumption with the beliefs the person held entering the period. “Prospective” gain-loss is the utility of news about future consumption (2009, pp. 910, 912–913). The 2006 and 2007 models are described as the special case in which prospective weights are zero, so that “it is only the contrast between contemporaneous consumption and prior expectations that generates sensations of gain and loss” (2009, p. 913).

The comparison rule changes. The person ranks old and new beliefs by percentile and feels \(\mu\) on the consumption-utility gap at each percentile (2009, p. 914):

\[ N^k(F^k_{t,\tau} \mid F^k_{t-1,\tau}) = \int_0^1 \mu\bigl(m^k(c_{F^k_{t,\tau}}(p)) - m^k(c_{F^k_{t-1,\tau}}(p))\bigr)\, dp. \]

They explicitly set aside applying the 2006 “each outcome against each possible outcome” cross-product to old beliefs versus new beliefs, because that “would imply that she experiences gain-loss utility even if she does not change her beliefs, which seems unrealistic” (2009, p. 914). \(\mu\) is again the Kahneman–Tversky value function, with the same A0–A4 (2009, pp. 914–915). The reference is still rational expectations in a preferred personal equilibrium, and they again say the utility function “can be combined with any theory of how those beliefs are formed” (2009, p. 915). On narrow bracketing of money, they say that attending to wealth changes can be “a manifestation of a preference over changes in beliefs” rather than a mistake, because wealth is news about future consumption (2009, p. 910).

Bottom line

People are assumed to feel gains and losses, in the Kahneman–Tversky sense, on top of ordinary consumption utility. Kőszegi and Rabin do not derive that feeling from rationality or from equilibrium. What they endogenize is the benchmark: it is the distribution the person recently expected, and in equilibrium that expectation has to match what she will actually do. If you handed her some other expectation from outside, she would still feel gains and losses against it. The new content, relative to prospect theory, is that the benchmark is her own recent beliefs, that those beliefs are pinned down by her planned behavior, and that a fully expected sure thing therefore carries no gain-loss utility. The 2007 paper is the same preference applied to risk, with probabilities kept linear. The 2009 paper adds that the feeling is triggered by news, including news about future consumption, by comparing old and new beliefs percentile by percentile.
